TITLE
-----
ZEROGUARD: A Solver-Free Battery Safety Certificate for Autonomous Vehicles,
from Robotaxis to Deep Space -- Anchored Collapse and the Geometry of the
Admissible Input Set

ABSTRACT (paste as plain text)
------------------------------
An autonomous vehicle's battery must be pushed hard enough to be useful and held back enough to
be safe, and the state deciding where that line falls -- series resistance, plating margin,
true capacity -- is not measurable in the field. Every unattended vehicle inherits the same
compromise: a fixed de-rate chosen offline for the worst cell at the worst temperature at end of
life, applied whatever the present state happens to be. The standard alternatives are to
identify the pack online, which needs excitation an operational vehicle cannot inject, or to
solve a constrained optimisation each step, whose iteration count a safety task slot cannot be
sized around.

We prove that neither is necessary. Terminal voltage, temperature and the lithium-plating
potential are each monotone in applied current -- the first two by the sign of the
overpotential and the strict positivity of I^2R, the third because the plating proxy is affine
and decreasing in current -- so the admissible input set is a single interval, and its
edges are found by bisection in a fixed number of one-step model evaluations, with no
optimiser, no convergence test, and no identification. The published form assumes the safe input
is zero, which holds for a cell being charged and fails for any vehicle doing its job. That
failure is a misdiagnosis: zero still satisfies every temperature and voltage constraint on a
discharging pack; what zero cannot do is serve the load, which is a floor rather than a
cap. Anchored Collapse adds the floor family and a second bisection -- 40
fixed evaluations against 20.

What that buys is not speed but transfer. Tuned on a fleet a manufacturer could
characterise in advance -- mild ambient, cells not yet aged -- the fastest safe fixed de-rate
is 1.2C. Deployed on the fleet a vehicle actually meets, from -10 to 40 C and
out to end of life, that same rate violates in 26.2 % of sessions. The
certificate, re-tuned not at all because it has nothing to tune, violates in
0 of 400. Matching its safety with a constant requires
dropping to 0.5C, and against that constant it still delivers
+2.0 % more charge. The honest claim is therefore parity-or-better on charge and
a categorical difference on safety, not the large speed-up a single chosen baseline suggests.
Against model-predictive control it returns the same answer -- to 0.66 SOC
points -- at 20 model evaluations against 644, and the 20 is a proved
bound while MPC's number is whatever its iteration cap allowed.
Across 16 platforms in four media and 61 experiments totalling
506,747 episodes, nothing was breached while the certificate reported feasible, the
admissible set was a single interval in all 64,000 dense scans, and worst-case latency was
123.9 \mus -- 1.24 % of a 10 ms ISO~26262 slot.

The comparison a control reader asks for first is against the CBF quadratic program, and it is
closer than a benchmark. Enforced on the true model instead of a linearisation, the discrete-time
CBF condition for a monotone constraint defines an interval -- single-valued in all
600 states tested -- so this method is the bisection that solves the CBF problem
exactly, with \gamma entering as a margin. The QP is its linearised approximation, and the
approximation is not free: given the exact plant and no margin, so that nothing else can absorb
the error, the QP breaches in 7.0 % of sessions at the matched condition \gamma = 1
where the bisection breaches in 0 of 600. Restoring its safety costs
8.5 SOC points.

The arrangement the method is for is a filter wrapping a controller it does not trust, and both
halves of that are measured. A policy fitted by cross-entropy search to maximise delivered charge
with no safety term breaches a certified constraint in 400 of 400
sessions on its own and in 0 through the filter, while still delivering
+16.7 SOC points more than the safe protocol it replaces. On a policy that was already
safe the filter intervenes in 0.39 % of steps, and across a swept request its
intervention is monotone and begins strictly before breaching does.

The two application claims that carry furthest are checked against physics we did not write and
against a compiler. Safety: the plating constraint the paper enforces but never certifies is
what stands between a cold fast charge and metallic lithium on the anode -- the first link in the
chain that ends in an internal short. Charging a DFN cell to a matched 70 % at
0 C, the rate a de-rate exists to forbid drives the anode potential negative in
2 of 8 sessions and deposits 20.4 mAh while it is there;
the certificate crosses in 0, and holds a wider anode margin than the fixed
de-rate at every ambient -- 19.8 minutes slower cold, where the constraint binds,
and 23.8 faster warm, where it does not. Deployability: the filter is not an estimate of a
footprint but a compiled one, 2,064 B of code and constants and 68 B of RAM, agreeing
with the reference on feasibility at all 20,000 states tested and never exceeding it by more
than one bisection quantum.

The value is not uniform across domains and the paper measures where it is not. Holding every
domain's incumbent to the same tune-then-deploy standard -- which the first version of this work
applied only to the ground baseline -- the incumbent rule fails in two of four media and the
certificate in none; in the other two the incumbent survives and still delivers more mission, and
\S\refsec:cross predicts which two in advance from whether closure is envelope- or
energy-driven. On the ground, scored on the quantity a fleet actually buys, the certificate
returns 17.4x as many vehicles to service inside a 40-minute window
-- while costing -29.7 points above 32.5 C ambient, where its own margin
exceeds the available headroom.

The plants are ours, which is the objection that matters, so the certificate is also run against a
Doyle-Fuller-Newman cell -- a different model class, with concentration gradients the
reduced-order model has no state for. Across 40 charging sessions it breached neither
certified constraint (peak 4.052 V against 4.20, 32.5 C against 45), and
the affine plating proxy the paper enforces but never certifies kept DFN's actual negative
electrode potential above the deposition onset in all of them. It holds with DFN transport
properties misspecified by 20x and gives way at 50x.

Two things make the guarantee checkable rather than asserted. It is machine-checked in
Lean~4 -- nine theorems, no \textttsorry, no dependency on any axiom -- and the
proofs turn out to need less than claimed: safety holds for an arbitrary predicate, so
monotonicity buys optimality, not safety. And the two assumptions are tested against 33
cells measured by another laboratory: resistance growth stays inside the assumed bound in
100.00 % of 1,255 measurements over the life the certificate claims, and heat
generation is confirmed monotone in current across 2,764 measured cycles
(rho = +0.714), model-free.

Five results contradicted the hypotheses they were written to test and are reported as findings:
the certificate refuses the calibrated cell for a rotorcraft before takeoff above
1.2C of hover draw; in a sealed hull the binding constraint is plating at every water
temperature -- the one constraint the theorem enforces but cannot certify; in deep space a
smaller radiator delivers more charge, the pack being cold-limited; under an ice shelf
closure leaves 7 minutes against a 20-minute transit, making closure
the wrong trigger; a fixed state-of-charge reserve, which we predicted would be badly
miscalibrated, is not beaten where the mission ends on charge; and in a pack driven to
divergence by those same EPA schedules, the cell that binds the certificate sits among the
coldest in the pack, not the hottest, because the plating cap tightens as a cell cools --
so the lumped pack model that every single-temperature simulation implicitly certifies against
breaks no certified constraint at all and plates 19.4 % of cells.
